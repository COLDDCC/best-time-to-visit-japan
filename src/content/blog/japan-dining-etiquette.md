Japanese dining has a reputation for being full of rules, which makes a lot of visitors more anxious than they need to be. The truth: locals are used to foreign guests and rarely expect perfection. But a handful of habits are genuinely worth learning, both out of respect and because they make the experience smoother.

## The two chopstick rules that actually matter

Almost every other "chopstick rule" you'll read online is minor, but these two connect directly to funeral customs and will visibly unsettle people at your table:

- **Never stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice.** It resembles incense sticks placed at a funeral altar.
- **Never pass food directly from your chopsticks to someone else's.** This mirrors a funeral ritual where cremated bones are passed between family members with chopsticks. If you want to share food, place it on the other person's plate instead.

Resting chopsticks across your bowl, using the wrong end to serve from a shared plate, or fumbling with them is genuinely fine — nobody will think twice.

## Say it before and after

"Itadakimasu" (roughly "I gratefully receive") before eating and "gochisousama deshita" ("thank you for the meal") when finished are small, common phrases — not required, but appreciated and easy to pick up after a day or two.

## Slurping noodles is normal, not rude

For ramen, soba, and udon, slurping is completely standard — it's said to cool the noodles and enhance the flavor, and locals do it themselves. Don't feel self-conscious about it.

## Izakaya basics

An izakaya (casual Japanese pub) usually seats you and brings a small appetizer (otoshi) automatically — this is a cover charge, not something you ordered, and it's normal to be charged a few hundred yen for it. Ordering is typically shared-plate style: order a few dishes at a time as you go, rather than everything up front. Tapping the table or calling "sumimasen" gets a server's attention; there's no tipping expected, and leaving cash on the table can actually cause confusion.

## Tipping isn't part of the culture

Japan doesn't have a tipping culture, in restaurants or otherwise. Good service is simply the standard, and leaving extra money can be awkward — some staff will chase you down to return it. Pay exactly what's on the bill.

## Splitting the bill

"Warikan" (splitting evenly) is common among friends, but many restaurants — especially smaller ones — prefer one person to pay at the register rather than splitting multiple cards or cash piles at the table. If you're in a larger group, it's often easiest to designate one payer and settle up separately afterward.

## The bottom line

Most of what feels intimidating about Japanese dining etiquette resolves itself just by watching what the table next to you is doing. Get the chopstick rules right, relax about the rest, and the food will do the rest of the work.
