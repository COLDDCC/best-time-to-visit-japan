A Japanese onsen (hot spring bath) is one of the most relaxing experiences in the country — and one of the easiest to get wrong on your first visit. None of the rules are complicated, but they're rarely spelled out for visitors, so here's what actually matters.

## Wash before you soak

The bath itself is for soaking, not cleaning. Every onsen has a row of low shower stations with stools, soap, and shampoo before you get anywhere near the tub. Sit down, rinse off, and wash thoroughly — hair included — before stepping in. Skipping this is the single most common mistake foreign visitors make, and it's the one locals notice fastest.

## Onsen are (almost always) naked, and gender-separated

Swimsuits aren't worn in the bath itself. Entrances are split by gender (look for red/pink for women, blue for men, or the kanji 女 and 男), and once inside, everyone bathes nude. This feels like the biggest hurdle for first-timers, but in practice nobody pays attention to anyone else — it's normalized and unremarkable to regular visitors.

## The small towel stays out of the water

You'll usually be given (or can rent) a small hand towel. Use it to wash, and you can hold it in front of you while walking around, but it does not go in the bathwater — most people fold it and set it on their head or beside the tub while soaking.

## Tattoos: check before you book

Many onsen and public bathhouses still refuse entry to visibly tattooed guests, a policy tied to the historical association between tattoos and organized crime rather than tattoos themselves. This is changing — some spots now allow tattoos, waterproof cover stickers, or private baths — but it's inconsistent enough that it's worth checking the specific facility's policy (or booking a ryokan with a private in-room bath) rather than assuming.

## Long hair goes up

If your hair is long enough to touch the water, tie it up. Hair (and the small towel) touching the bath water is considered unclean by the other bathers who'll use it after you.

## Take it slow

Onsen water is genuinely hot — often 40-44°C (104-111°F) — noticeably hotter than a typical home bath. Ease in gradually, don't stay in longer than feels comfortable, and get out if you feel lightheaded. Skip the onsen entirely if you've been drinking heavily beforehand; alcohol plus hot water is a bad combination.

## The reward

Get the basics right and an onsen is genuinely one of the best parts of a Japan trip — especially after a day of hiking or a long Shinkansen ride. Ryokan with rotenburo (outdoor baths) overlooking mountains or the sea are worth planning a whole night around.
